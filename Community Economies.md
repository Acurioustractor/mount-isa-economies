Designing a Self-Sustaining Local Service Economy in Mount Isa
Imagine Mount Isa as a living ecosystem of services and relationships. The goal is to keep money, skills, and trust circulating within the community, rather than draining out. Below we prototype a town-level economic intelligence system that helps locals see where money comes in, where it leaks out, and where new local opportunities could replace external spending. Using Mount Isa (a remote mining city of ~19,000 in Queensland) as an example, we explore how to map the community fabric, trace economic flows, identify import replacements, and build a resilient local economy.
Community Fabric: The Invisible Engines of Care and Service
Every community runs on numerous essential but often invisible contributions. In Mount Isa, as in other towns, a mix of formal workers and informal caregivers keep the town thriving:
Formal Care & Maintenance Workers: Nurses, teachers, council maintenance crews, local shopkeepers, etc., who provide health, education, and daily services. They meet critical needs (healthcare, schooling, food) yet are sometimes undervalued by markets (e.g. aged care workers).
Informal and Voluntary Caregivers: Families raising children, Indigenous elders sharing cultural knowledge, neighbors who watch out for each other, volunteers running sports clubs and events. This “quiet economy” of care and community work is vital but not counted in GDP. (Historically, economists like Adam Smith even deemed domestic and care labor “unproductive,” rendering much of women’s and community work invisible in economic metrics
GitHub
.)
These people meet needs that conventional economics often overlooks: keeping kids safe, maintaining community cohesion, preserving culture, and responding to crises. For instance, an Aboriginal elder mentoring youth in Mount Isa or a retiree volunteering at the local footy club provides stability and wellbeing that no external fly-in service can replicate. Recognizing these roles is step one – our local economic system should start by mapping who is doing this work of care and maintenance. A simple community survey or forum can identify the network of caregivers, volunteers, and local “fixers” (mechanics, handy-people, etc.) who form the backbone of Mount Isa’s resilience. These are the assets to build on.
Economic Flows: Tracing a Dollar Through Town
Next, we follow the money. Where does an average dollar earned in Mount Isa go, and how much leaks out of the local bucket? Consider a mine worker earning wages in town. Say he spends $1:
Local Expenditure: He might buy lunch at a family-run cafe or pay a local barber. Those dollars largely stay in the bucket of the local economy – the cafe owner pays local staff and suppliers, who in turn spend in town. On average, about 48¢ of every $1 spent at a local independent business is recirculated locally, via employee wages, local purchases, and profits spent by local owners
reclaimdemocracy.org
.
Leakages to External Vendors: Now consider when he spends money at businesses that aren’t locally rooted. Paying his electricity bill, for example, sends money to an out-of-town utility company – a leak in the bucket
smartcitiesdive.com
. Ordering supplies from an online retailer is even worse: effectively 0% stays local, as online giants hoover money out without returning anything
smartcitiesdive.com
. Indeed, studies show chain stores recirculate only ~14% locally (out of each dollar), compared to ~48% for local firms
reclaimdemocracy.org
. In general, money spent at a local independent shop generates 3–5 times more local wealth than money spent at a corporate chain or online
smartcitiesdive.com
.
Using the “leaky bucket” analogy from the New Economics Foundation: the local economy is like a bucket – every time residents buy from outside or send payments to non-local entities, money pours out through holes in the bucket
smartcitiesdive.com
. The key is to plug those leaks. For example, if many households are paying outsiders for home repairs, could local tradespeople fill that need? If the hospital flies in a specialist doctor monthly, could we fund a local practitioner or telehealth solution? Trace one dollar in Mount Isa: it might start as wages from Glencore (the mining company) into a worker’s pocket. Part of it gets spent at a local grocery (stays in town), part at Kmart or a chain grocery (profits leave town), some on rent or mortgage (if the landlord or bank is outside, that money exits), some on a FIFO contractor (fly-in worker who earns in Isa but spends elsewhere), and some on local services (like a local mechanic or dentist) which keep money circulating. If we aggregate these patterns, we can estimate leakage. For instance, Mount Isa’s economy historically relies on FIFO mining workers – incomes earned here often get spent in Brisbane or Townsville. In 2022, there were about 1,500 more jobs located in Mount Isa than there were local residents employed (11,749 jobs vs 10,191 working residents)
mountisa.qld.gov.au
, implying many roles are filled by non-residents and their earnings likely flow out. Moreover, large retailers and construction contractors operating in town may repatriate profits to city headquarters. Economic “bridges” to nowhere: These are the spending areas where money flows out because no local alternative exists yet. Common examples include specialized trades or professional services: e.g. if no local electrician is available, people hire one from out of town (bridge for money to exit); if the council contracts a roadworks firm from Brisbane, or a major retailer sources all stock from interstate, that’s money leaving. Our task is to identify these bridges and replace them with local links. For each major category of leakage (whether it’s haircuts, construction, food, or energy), we ask: Could a local person or business provide this? Sometimes the answer is straightforward. In one small town case, residents drove 40 miles to get haircuts, taking the opportunity to also shop at big-box stores out of town
archive.strongtowns.org
archive.strongtowns.org
. The solution was to invite a barber to set up locally one day a week – giving everyone one less reason to spend money elsewhere
archive.strongtowns.org
archive.strongtowns.org
. This partial “import replacement” kept wealth circulating locally via that new barber service. Mount Isa, being larger, already has barbers; but we can apply the same logic to any service not currently met locally. Every dollar not leaked is a dollar that can respin through multiple local hands, multiplying its impact each time.
Replacement and Reinvestment: Plugging Leaks with Local Capability
Once we see where money is leaking, we can strategize how to replace critical external expenditures with local capacity. This is essentially practicing what urbanist Jane Jacobs called “import replacement”: systematically producing inside the community what it currently must import
archive.strongtowns.org
. Instead of chasing outside companies to “invest” (who often extract profits), we grow our own solutions. Identify high-impact leaks: Start by listing the biggest categories of outside spending. For Mount Isa, possibilities include:
Skilled trades and maintenance: If mines or council projects fly in electricians, heavy machinery mechanics, or construction crews, that’s a gap to fill locally.
Health and social services: Are specialists (doctors, therapists) only visiting from elsewhere? Perhaps local training or tele-services could plug this.
Retail goods: How much do residents spend online or at chain stores for things that could be sourced locally (food, clothing, hardware)? Even encouraging local stores to stock what people currently buy online can stem the outflow.
Utilities & energy: Money spent on grid electricity or fuel leaves town. Could local renewable energy or fuel alternatives (e.g. a community solar farm) replace some of that?
For each category, we evaluate feasibility to localize. Some needs can be met by incubating new local businesses or co-ops. For example, if a lot of construction contracts go to outside firms, we could establish a local construction cooperative or upskill local builders to bid for those contracts. If residents are buying produce from far away, support could be given to community agriculture or local suppliers. Crucially, this requires investment in people and tools. Here’s where a community investment pool comes in. The town can organize a fund (via a cooperative, credit union, or development trust) that pulls local savings or grants together to finance local enterprise and training. Imagine a community-owned fund that offers low-interest loans or grants to locals who want to train as plumbers, or to a group of entrepreneurs starting a local food hub. This is essentially investing the community’s money in its own capacity – turning leakages into opportunities. There are real examples to draw on:
Community Reinvestment Funds: Some regions create local development funds for exactly this purpose. For instance, community banks in Australia (partnered with Bendigo Bank) pool local shareholders’ capital to run a local branch, and reinvest profits into the town. Since 1998, Bendigo’s community bank model has channeled over A$366 million back into local projects nationwide
bendigobank.com.au
bendigobank.com.au
. All those funds – generated by local banking customers – are returned directly to the communities that generated them
bendigobank.com.au
, funding training, infrastructure, and services that would otherwise require outside money.
Cooperative Enterprises: Cooperative business models let community members jointly invest and own a needed service. A shining example is Hepburn Wind in rural Victoria, Australia’s first community-owned wind farm. Over 2,000 local people pooled small investments (raising ~$10 million) to build two wind turbines
cer.gov.au
. Now they generate power for 2,000 homes and keep energy revenue in the local area. This shows how a town can replace paying an outside power company with owning its own energy infrastructure. The co-op not only provides cheaper clean electricity but also puts profits into a community fund for local projects
theenergy.co
.
Anchor Institution Procurement: Another strategy is working with big local spenders (schools, hospitals, government offices) to redirect their procurement locally. In the UK, the famous “Preston Model” did this by getting city institutions to shift contracts to local suppliers. Over a few years, Preston’s public bodies went from spending 14% of their budgets locally to 28%, redirecting an extra £4 million a year into local firms
theguardian.com
. Since 2013, that approach has kept over £70 million circulating in Preston’s economy that used to leak out
cles.org.uk
 – a dramatic recapture of local wealth. Mount Isa could do similarly: e.g. encourage the City Council, hospital, and big employers to prefer local contractors and vendors, even if it means breaking contracts into smaller pieces that local businesses can handle.
To fund these efforts, our community investment pool might take multiple forms: a cooperative investment fund, crowd-sourced capital campaigns for specific needs (like a community-owned grocery store), or partnerships with credit unions for micro-loans. The organizational piece is key – it might be a local economic development board or an Indigenous-controlled trust (reflecting that Mount Isa sits on Kalkadoon country and prioritizing Indigenous enterprise is vital). The fund would target spending gaps: e.g., provide a grant to buy tools for a local auto-electrician shop so the mines don’t have to fly someone in, or offer a stipend for a nursing student on condition they return to practice in the community. By reinvesting local money in local people, we create a virtuous cycle: each formerly external service replaced by a local one means more jobs, more skills, and more money staying in town to be reinvested again.
Data and Story: Tools to Trace Flows and Engage the Community
To guide all these decisions, we need good data and a way to tell the story. A “community economic observatory” dashboard can visualize where money flows, where gaps exist, and track progress. Fortunately, there are open-source and low-cost tools that can help a community map these complex dynamics:
Money Flow Mapping: We can use open data and software to trace financial flows. For example, the New Economics Foundation developed a simple tool called Local Multiplier 3 (LM3) to measure how income is spent and re-spent in a local area
smartcitiesdive.com
smartcitiesdive.com
. A community group could run an LM3 analysis on, say, the local council’s budget or the mine’s wage spending – seeing what percentage ultimately stays local. This involves surveys or data on who the council’s suppliers are (and their location) and how employees spend their salaries. The result is a quantitative picture of the multiplier effect: e.g. a finding that “$1 of council spending with local businesses generates $0.45 in secondary local spending, vs $0.14 if spent with an external supplier”
reclaimdemocracy.org
. Such insights make the case for change in an evidence-based way.
Open-Source Dashboards: There are many free or open-source platforms to visualize data. A tech-savvy volunteer or local student could set up a simple web dashboard using tools like D3.js or Chart.js (JavaScript libraries) or an open-source BI tool (e.g. Metabase or Superset) to display key indicators. This dashboard could show Sankey diagrams of money flows (illustrating leaks), maps of service coverage, and charts of local vs external spending by category. For example, a Sankey chart might depict $100 entering Mount Isa’s economy from mining, then splitting into streams: $X to local payroll, $Y to outside contractors, $Z to local businesses, etc.
GIS and Service Mapping: Building on the existing Mount Isa Community Platform (which already maps services and uses AI to identify gaps), we can integrate economic data. For instance, layering on data like “number of local electricians per 1000 households” vs “how much is spent on electrical services” could highlight opportunities. Open-source mapping tools (like QGIS or Leaflet JS) could be used to create interactive maps of the local supply chain – showing which local needs are well served and which rely on imports.
Open Data Sources: A lot of data can be gathered with some coding elbow grease. We could scrape ABN registry or business directories to find which businesses in Mount Isa are locally owned vs branches of chains. We could use open government data on procurement (if available) to see contracts awarded to out-of-area firms. Even anonymized bank transaction data (if a local bank partners) could show aggregate consumer spending patterns (e.g. how much spent on Amazon or at big supermarkets vs local stores). All this can feed the observatory.
Importantly, this shouldn’t be a dry exercise – it’s about telling the community’s economic story in an engaging way. Data becomes more powerful when young people are involved in creating and communicating it. Here are ways to involve youth (and simultaneously build skills):
Youth Tech & Data Teams: Mount Isa’s high school and TAFE students could take part in collecting data and building the dashboard. For example, a student project might involve interviewing local businesses about where they buy their supplies (on- or off-island) and visualizing the results. Coding clubs or IT classes could contribute to the software – building web apps that show money flows. This gives students real-world experience in data science and civic tech.
Youth in Trades & Services: Not all young people will code – many might be more interested in hands-on trades. The economic observatory can point to which trades are in demand. The community could create apprenticeship pipelines for youths in areas like plumbing, solar installation, nursing aide, etc., guided by the identified service gaps. These young people become the future providers plugging leaks. For instance, if the dashboard shows a lack of local electricians, a partnership with a vocational college could recruit local youth into electrician training, funded by the community investment pool (scholarships or wage subsidies).
Storytelling and Media: Young folks could also help tell the success stories. They might run a social media campaign or podcast about “Local Legends” – interviewing the new local barber or the cooperative that started a new enterprise. By framing the narrative themselves, they build pride and awareness among peers. The data dashboard could even be run by a youth committee that presents findings at community meetings.
Finally, we must measure not just economic return but social wellbeing. A truly self-sustaining local economy improves quality of life, not just income. So our system should track some wellbeing indicators: e.g. employment rates (especially for youth and marginalized groups), average personal income staying in the community, but also social metrics like volunteerism rates, community trust (perhaps measured via surveys), and access to services (e.g. “90% of residents have a GP within 5 km now, up from 60% before”). We might incorporate a Community Wellbeing Index – combining economic metrics with health, education, cultural vitality indicators. For example, the dashboard might show that after localizing 10% of the economy, not only did local GDP or income go up, but community satisfaction (as polled) rose and out-migration of youth fell. Social impact can also be illustrated through stories: e.g. “Thanks to the local investment fund, Jane trained as a midwife and returned to Mount Isa – 50 more babies were delivered locally this year, and young mums feel more supported.” In essence, the numbers should be paired with human stories to capture improvements in both prosperity and belonging.
Prototype the Future: A One-Year, 10% Localization Pilot
How might all this work in practice? Let’s sketch a one-year pilot project for Mount Isa to localize 10% of its economy (i.e. reduce leakages by ten percentage points). A 10% shift may sound small, but evidence shows even that can have big impacts – communities have calculated that a 10% change in local purchasing can generate substantial new local jobs and wealth
reclaimdemocracy.org
. Here’s what the pilot could look like:
Launch a Community Economic Observatory (CEO) Team: Assemble a local taskforce including council officials, Indigenous leaders, business owners, elders, youth representatives, and perhaps a partnership with a nearby university or the Mount Isa Mines. This team is responsible for driving the pilot. They kick off by sharing data from the new dashboard – showing current leakages and target areas for a 10% improvement.
Identify Target Leakages: Using the data, the team pinpoints a few key sectors to localize. For example, they might find that tens of millions are leaving via outside building contractors and via household shopping online. So they choose Construction/Handyman Services and Retail/Food as two focus areas to improve by 10%. Another focus might be Local Procurement by Institutions (e.g. get the hospital and council to shift 10% more of their contracts locally).
Mobilize Local Providers & Talent: The team works to recruit or develop local capacity in those target areas. For construction, they could help form a network of local tradies to jointly bid on larger projects, or seed-fund a new worker cooperative (perhaps several young apprentices under a retired builder’s guidance). For retail, they coordinate a “Buy Local” campaign challenging residents to do 10% more of their shopping at local markets and stores instead of online or out-of-town. If necessary, they assist a local entrepreneur to expand inventory to meet needs (e.g. a new local grocery co-op to carry products people were importing). Community investment funds are tapped here – e.g. offering mini-grants for local business expansions, or zero-interest loans for entrepreneurs to purchase equipment.
Training and Jobs Program: Parallel to business support, the pilot funds training for local people to fill skill gaps. Perhaps they sponsor 5 young people to get certified in a needed trade (with a guarantee of job placement locally), and bring in a short-term expert to train local employees in specialized tasks the mine usually outsources. This addresses the skills pipeline so that by year’s end there are, say, 3 new local electricians or an IT technician in town.
Community Engagement & Storytelling: Throughout the year, the effort is highly public and inclusive. Regular town-hall meetings or barbecues are held where the dashboard is updated live – showing, for example, “Local spending has increased from 50% to 55% – we’re halfway to our 10% goal!” The team celebrates success stories: like a local farm that started supplying the school cafeteria (replacing a previous shipped-in supplier) or a family that started a plumbing business and hired two formerly unemployed locals. These human stories are featured in local media and at community gatherings, building momentum and pride.
Track Metrics & Outcomes: The observatory continuously measures the impact. By year’s end, they evaluate results: Did we achieve a 10% reduction in leakages? For example, if Mount Isa’s economy was leaking, say, $100 million per year, did we keep an extra $10 million circulating locally? Metrics might include: number of new local contracts won (that used to go outside), increase in local business revenue, reduction in unemployment, etc. Social outcomes are also noted (e.g. crime rates fell or school retention improved, if relevant, which some attribute to better local job prospects and morale).
Reflect and Institutionalize: The pilot concludes with a community report and celebration. The findings show not only numbers but testimonials: success stories that illustrate resilience and belonging. For instance: “Local electrician John was able to quit FIFO work and start a business here at home – now our electrical maintenance contract for the mine stays in Mount Isa, creating 5 local jobs and a reliable service”. Or “Our youth coding team built an app that shows residents all local shops for various products – they increased local shopping by $2 million this year!”. These narratives make the data come alive. Seeing more faces they recognize on the supply side of the economy, residents feel a greater sense of community efficacy.
In zooming out on the dashboard after one year, we aim to see new patterns of resilience, self-sufficiency and social connection. Visually, the money-flow Sankey diagrams would show thicker loops staying within town, and thinner lines exiting. Perhaps a network graph of local businesses and trades would appear denser, with more local-to-local transactions, indicating that businesses are sourcing from each other instead of distant suppliers. We’d see more dots on the map representing new local services, filling prior gaps (e.g. a new childcare center cooperative, a solar installer, a local courier service handling online orders for local shops). On the social side, the community survey might show improved trust and optimism – people feeling like “we have each other’s backs” and can solve problems without waiting for outsiders. Ideally, youth who participated see a future for themselves in Mount Isa, and elders see their efforts valued. In short, the pilot’s zoomed-out view should display a tighter-knit local economy: one where money circulates in a virtuous cycle, skills are grown and passed on, and economic decisions are made with community wellbeing in mind.
Toward a Nationwide Movement: Community Economic Observatories Everywhere 🌏
For a bonus thought experiment – imagine every town in the country had its own community economic observatory and wealth-building routine. What data, tools, and rituals would make it work, and how could Mount Isa pioneer it?
Data Required: Towns would need access to granular economic data. This includes local business directories, employment data, spending patterns, and public procurement records. Open data from government (like regional accounts, or big employers disclosing local vs non-local spend) can feed these observatories. There’s also a role for citizen-generated data: communities could conduct their own surveys (e.g. “Where do you do your shopping? What skills do you have that are underused?”). A standard template for data collection could help towns gather comparable info. Privacy is important – data should be aggregated (we care about totals and flows, not individuals’ finances).
Tools & Technology: A simple, replicable dashboard platform would empower towns. One could envision an open-source software package (perhaps maintained by a national civic tech initiative) that any community can deploy. It might integrate a database (for inputting local data like business lists or survey results) and a visualization interface (charts, maps). The UI should be simple and visual – e.g. color-coded maps of money outflows, or dials showing “% local procurement”. Ideally, this is hosted accessibly (perhaps a web app each community can customize). Low-cost tools like Google Sheets + free visualization APIs might suffice for smaller communities, whereas bigger ones might use more advanced analytics. The key is transparency and simplicity – everyone should be able to understand the charts of where the money goes.
Community Rituals: Data alone won’t change things; it’s about how people use it. Towns could establish regular forums or “money meetings” – perhaps a quarterly town hall where the community economic dashboard is reviewed. This could become as routine as a council meeting, but more engaging – like a community hackathon or a potluck where alongside socializing, folks discuss the latest insights (“Hey, looks like we spend $5M a year on external cleaning services – could we create a local cleaning coop?”). Storytelling sessions would be a ritual too: each meeting highlights one local hero or success in plugging leaks. Another ritual could be an annual “local economy report card” – perhaps even a friendly competition between towns on certain metrics (sparking incentive to improve, in a cooperative way). Schools could integrate this into curriculum, with students presenting their town’s economic data at an assembly.
Network of Observatories: If many towns run their own observatories, they can share knowledge. Mount Isa could play a pioneering role by developing the model and documenting the process – essentially writing the “Community Wealth-Building Playbook” for others to follow. Perhaps Mount Isa partners with a similar rural town in another state to compare notes and refine the approach. Eventually, a national network or alliance (supported by organizations like CLES in the UK or Ethical Fields in Australia
ethicalfields.com
) could emerge to provide guidance, training, and maybe even funding. This network might host an online portal where each community uploads their data and stories – a bit like how open data observatories exist for environmental data, but here for local economic vitality.
Institutional Support: Over time, state or federal governments might support these local observatories by aligning policy incentives. For instance, grants could be given to communities that demonstrably increase local multipliers or establish investment pools. Just as we track unemployment or GDP, a “local retention index” could become a common metric. The observatories could feed into larger policy via aggregated data, while still keeping community control at the ground level.
Mount Isa’s Role: Mount Isa’s initiative can inspire others by showing tangible results in a challenging context (remote, resource-dependent, with Indigenous communities). If it succeeds in localizing 10% of its economy and improving outcomes, it can be a flagship case. Mount Isa could host a “Community Economy Summit” inviting other towns to see the dashboard, meet the locals involved, and learn hands-on. In doing so, Mount Isa not only improves itself but also honors the principle of self-determination – demonstrating how a community can take charge of its economic destiny and then help other communities do the same.
In summary, designing a self-sustaining local service economy is about seeing the big picture of flows and the small stories of people. It requires maps and data, but also trust and collaboration. By mapping who already cares for the community, tracing where each dollar goes, and mobilizing collective investment to plug leaks, a town like Mount Isa can strengthen its ecosystem. The result will be more money staying in town, more jobs and skills for locals, and a tighter weave of relationships – in short, a more resilient, flourishing community. And if one town can do it, why not many? As the saying goes, don’t fly in and fly out expecting to solve our problems – the solutions are within us, waiting to be connected and unleashed.
